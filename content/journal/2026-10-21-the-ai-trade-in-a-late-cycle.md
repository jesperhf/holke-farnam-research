This pulls the series together: the [sector map](the-ai-trade-and-the-business-cycle.html) and the company work-ups on [memory and storage](memory-and-storage.html), [power and chip equipment](power-and-chip-equipment.html) and [optical networking](optical-networking.html). My view going in: the AI trade is a set of different sectors, none of them looks attractive at today's prices once you factor in where we are in the business cycle, and corporate earnings are what is holding the economy up. When those fold over, we could be heading into a contraction.

## Executive summary

**The AI trade is many sectors priced as one bet: that capex keeps rising. At today's prices none of them pays enough for the risk that it doesn't, and the economy now depends on that same bet.** The data back that read on direction. They also say the turn has not started yet, which argues for a priced watchlist rather than positions or shorts.

- **Earnings are holding the economy up.** Profits are at their highest share of GDP since the 1940s and AI investment is about a third of 2026 growth, while hiring has stalled (+29k in September) and real wages are falling.
- **Those earnings are narrow.** Micron and Nvidia alone add 9 of the 29 points of S&P 500 Q3 earnings growth, and energy is riding an oil shock. Wealthy households, half of consumer spending, depend on the stock prices those earnings support.
- **11 of the 12 companies I modeled trade above my base-case value.** The exception, SK hynix, is in memory, the part of the chain most exposed to a turn. The eight long-term names return 7.4% to 9.5% a year in my base case, only 2 to 4 points over a 5.31% Treasury, and the 12 fall 47% to 84% in my bear cases.
- **The market is expensive on margins, not multiples.** The S&P 500's 19x forward P/E is near its averages; the risk is that record margins are the peak.
- **What would confirm a contraction:** jobless claims above 250k, ISM below 50, a hyperscaler guiding 2027 capex flat or down, and DRAM contract prices falling.
- **What to do:** no new positions. Buy TSMC below $345, nVent below $110 and Fabrinet below $280, treating them as one AI position rather than three. ASML, Vertiv and SK hynix only in a deeper selloff.

## The macro picture: earnings are doing the heavy lifting

**The data support the thesis. Corporate profits and AI investment are carrying an economy whose other engines are slowing.** Real GDP grew 1.5% annualized in Q2, yet after-tax profits reached 19.4% of gross value added, the highest since the series began in the 1940s, and rose about 9% in the quarter alone ([KuCoin, citing BEA](https://www.kucoin.com/news/flash/us-corporate-profits-rise-10-in-q2-2026-highest-margins-since-1940s)).

| Engine | Latest reading | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Corporate profits | S&P 500 Q3 EPS growth 29.0%; net margin 14.9% vs a 12.6% five-year average ([FactSet](https://advantage.factset.com/hubfs/Website/Resources%20Section/Research%20Desk/Earnings%20Insight/EarningsInsight_101626.pdf)) | Near record, now the main support |
| AI investment | About a third of US GDP growth in 2026; 36% of Q2 growth net of imports ([ING via Hellenic Shipping News](https://www.hellenicshippingnews.com/how-much-is-ai-contributing-to-us-economic-growth/)) | Still rising, increasingly debt-funded |
| Wealthy consumers | Top 10% of earners made up half of consumer spending in 2025 ([Pepperstone, citing Moody's](https://pepperstone.com/en-au/insights/market-analysis/economic-indicators/the-k-shaped-economy-explains-us-resilience-despite-stalling-labour-market)); AI-driven stock gains add about 0.44 points to GDP through spending (ING) | Tied to stock prices |
| Everyone else | Wage growth 3.0%, lowest since May 2021 ([The Trading Tools](https://www.thetrading.tools/daily-brief/2026-10-02)), below 3.7% CPI inflation: real wages are falling | Squeezed |
| Jobs | September payrolls +29k; unemployment 4.2% | Cooling |
| Policy | Fed hiked to 3.75% to 4.00% in September; 10-year at 5.31% | Tightening |

**The earnings themselves are narrow.** Excluding just Micron and Nvidia, S&P 500 Q3 growth falls from 29% to 20%. Excluding Nvidia and Meta, the Magnificent 7 grow only 7.5%. Energy, up 128%, is riding the oil shock. So the profit engine depends on two price-driven cycles, memory chips and oil, plus AI capex ([FactSet](https://advantage.factset.com/hubfs/Website/Resources%20Section/Research%20Desk/Earnings%20Insight/EarningsInsight_101626.pdf)).

**One correction to the common view.** The S&P 500 trades at 19.0 times forward earnings, slightly below its 5-year (19.8) and 10-year (19.1) averages. The market is not expensive on multiples; it is expensive on margins. The risk is that the "E" is at a cyclical peak, not that the "P/E" is stretched. Analysts already expect growth to slow to 16% in 2027.

## The weak link: an expansion running on one engine

**The contraction case is the better-supported one on direction; the timing signals say the turn has not started yet.** Every link in the left column is at or near a record, and each one depends on the link above it. When capex growth slows, the reversal travels down the same chain, and the labor market at the bottom has little cushion left.

| --- | --- |
| Earnings growth is narrow: two semis and an oil shock do most of the work | Initial jobless claims at 197k: companies are not laying off yet |

**What this means for positioning.** A contraction thesis does not need to be right on timing to justify patience. At today's prices, the reward for being early in AI stocks is small (see the next sections), while the cost of being wrong in a downturn is large. The signals to watch for the turn are claims above 250k, ISM below 50, and the first hyperscaler capex guide that is flat or down.

## The AI value chain, sector by sector

**Every layer is priced for the build to keep going, and the layers I valued in detail all fail my return test.** The [sector map](the-ai-trade-and-the-business-cycle.html) split the AI trade into eleven sub-sectors. I built company models for four of them ([memory](memory-and-storage.html), [power and chip equipment](power-and-chip-equipment.html), [optics](optical-networking.html)); the other seven are judged from the sector work, without models.

| Sub-sector | How I judged it | Late-cycle verdict | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Power and thermal equipment | 3 models: Vertiv, nVent, Siemens Energy | Expensive; watchlist nVent | Base values 20% to 40% below price. nVent is the steadiest, with about 60% of sales outside data centers |
| Semiconductor equipment and foundry | 3 models: TSMC, ASML, Applied Materials | Expensive; watchlist TSMC | TSMC is closest to fair value (19% above base). Equipment orders fell 44% to 46% in 2009 |
| Networking and optics | 2 models: Coherent, Fabrinet | Expensive; watchlist Fabrinet | Coherent is spending ahead of demand with negative free cash flow |
| Memory and storage | 4 models: Micron, SK hynix, Seagate, Western Digital | Most exposed to a turn | Pricing power is real through 2028, but prices are set by the rate of change and supply always arrives |
| Custom silicon (Broadcom, Marvell) | Sector work only | Priced for contracted growth | Best revenue visibility in semis, but concentrated in a few buyers who can cut orders together |
| Merchant GPUs (Nvidia, AMD) | Sector work only | Consensus, priced for it | About $5.5T of value; vendor financing of customers is a late-cycle signal |
| Power generation and nuclear | Sector work only | Most defensive of the AI plays | Long contracts protect cash flow; political pressure on power bills caps the upside |
| Hyperscalers | Sector work only | Most resilient, not cheap | Capex now absorbs most operating cash flow and debt is rising; cash-rich names hold up best |
| Neoclouds and data center developers | Sector work only | Avoid | The leverage in the system lives here; refinancing at 5%+ rates is the central risk |
| Software and AI applications | Sector work only | The one place with valuation support | Already de-rated on fears of AI disruption, so the burden is proof of pricing, not price |
| Physical AI and edge | Sector work only | Avoid for now | Mostly private leaders and little revenue; memory costs hurt device makers |

**The pattern.** The further a sector sits from the end customer, the more its earnings depend on capex staying high, and the more it falls when capex pauses. The suppliers I modeled sit at that end. The two layers that could hold up in a contraction, cash-rich hyperscalers and contracted power generation, are resilient rather than cheap, and software is the only layer where the market has already priced in a bad outcome.

**A caveat on my own read.** The "none are attractive" verdict is backed by numbers for the 12 companies I modeled. For the seven sectors I did not model, it is a judgment from the sector work, and software in particular has not been tested against a valuation.

## Valuation: today's prices already pay for the bull case

{{chart:value-vs-price}}

**Buying at today's prices earns roughly what a bond pays plus a thin premium.** If my base cases play out, the eight long-term names (power, chip equipment, optics) return 7.4% to 9.5% a year from today. With the 10-year Treasury at 5.31%, that is a premium of 2 to 4 points for owning some of the most cyclical businesses in the market, against the 4 to 5 points that would normally compensate for that risk. In my bear cases, the 12 names fall 47% to 84%.

**SK hynix is the only name below base value, and it is the wrong kind of exception for this view.** Its 7% gap to base value depends on memory prices staying high through 2028. In a contraction, memory is the first place prices break, and its bear case is −47%. It stays a watchlist name.

**Why the discount rate matters more than the story.** Each extra point of discount rate cuts these values by 10% to 15%. On the calculated CAPM rates rather than my assumed ones, every base value falls further below price (TSMC to $303, Vertiv to $107). The market is valuing these companies as if the cost of capital were well below what a 5.3% Treasury implies.

## Three ways the next two years could go

**Two of the three paths take AI stocks well below today's prices, and the third only earns a bond-like return.** The weights are my judgment, not a model output.

|  | 1. The build keeps compounding | 2. Capex digestion, no recession | 3. Profit-led contraction (my main worry) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| My weight | 30% | 40% | 30% |
| What happens | AI revenue arrives fast enough to fund capex; earnings broaden beyond semis; the Fed holds | Hyperscalers pause orders in 2027 to 2028 as in the 2023 chip downturn; the economy slows but avoids layoffs | Capex cuts hit supplier earnings, stocks fall, top-earner spending slows and firms cut jobs to protect margins |
| AI supplier stocks | Close to my base cases: 7% to 10% a year from today | Toward my bear cases on the weaker names; quality names fall to around their buy-below prices, about 20% to 35% down | My bear cases: 47% to 84% below today's prices |
| What to watch | Cloud revenue growth speeding up; software firms charging for AI agents | Flat capex guides; memory contract prices turning down; order backlogs shrinking | Jobless claims above 250k; ISM below 50; credit spreads widening on AI debt |
| What it means for me | I miss some upside, partly offset by 4% to 5.3% on cash and Treasuries | Watchlist prices fill | Buy prices fill early, and much lower prices follow |

**The asymmetry is the case for waiting.** In the good path, staying out costs the gap between a 7% to 10% stock return and a 4% to 5% risk-free return. In the other two, staying out avoids losses of 20% to 80%. That trade-off favors patience even if you put a lower weight on the contraction than I do.

## What would make this view wrong

**The strongest case against me is that the market is not expensive on multiples, and the layoffs that end expansions have not started.** These are the developments that would make me revisit the call.

- **AI starts paying for itself.** If cloud and software revenue from AI grows fast enough to fund capex from cash flow rather than debt, the chain in my diagram stops being fragile. Watch cloud growth rates and whether software firms can charge for AI agents.
- **Rates fall.** Each point off the discount rate adds 10% to 15% to my values. A 10-year near 4% would close more than half the gap to fair value for TSMC, nVent and Fabrinet without any price change.
- **Earnings broaden.** If S&P 500 growth outside semis and energy picks up in 2027, the profit engine is less narrow than it looks today. Analysts already expect 16% EPS growth next year.
- **The labor market holds.** Claims at 197k and prime-age employment near 80.7% say companies are keeping workers. A profit squeeze that does not become layoffs is a slowdown, not a contraction.
- **My models are too cautious.** The base cases are bear-leaning by design. If AI demand stays at current rates through 2030, the bull cases apply, and five of the twelve names have bull values above today's price.

**The cost of being early.** Late-cycle expansions can run longer than the data suggest, and the cost of waiting is the gap between stock returns and the 4% to 5.3% that cash and Treasuries pay meanwhile. That is a modest cost against the downside in the other two scenarios, but it is not zero.
